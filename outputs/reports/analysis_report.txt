## 1. CT slice quality control

  outputs/reports/ct_qc_slice_trends.png

  This figure asks whether measurements change suspiciously as the scanner moves through Z.

  - Z-slice robust range: This is the 95th–5th percentile intensity range in each slice. The repeated peaks probably correspond largely to real periodic
    lattice geometry passing through the slices. The much larger behavior near the beginning and end reflects specimen/end-region effects or changing
    material coverage.

  - Abrupt-jump robust z: Measures unusually large slice-to-slice changes in mean intensity. Values above the red line are flagged. The pipeline identified
    111 slices, but their periodic organization suggests the detector is responding to lattice periodicity as well as potential acquisition
    discontinuities.

  - Low-contrast robust z: Looks for slices whose intensity range is abnormally small. Nothing exceeds the threshold, so no slices were classified as
    anomalously low contrast.

  Significance: There is no evidence of an extended low-contrast failure, but slice-to-slice intensity is highly structured. The 111 abrupt flags should
  not be interpreted as 111 defective CT slices without visually checking them; this detector does not distinguish scanner artifacts from genuine periodic
  geometry.

  ## 2. Segmentation diagnostics

  outputs/reports/ct_segmentation_diagnostics.png

  This is the main check that bright CT material was converted into the correct binary lattice.

  - Normalized CT, central Z: The intensity-normalized source slice. Struts appear as bright lines or point-like intersections depending on how they cross
    the selected plane.

  - Selected ensemble mask: The final binary foreground after combining three segmentation candidates by a two-of-three vote and retaining the largest
    connected component.

  - Segmentation overlay: The green contour shows the mask boundary on the CT. Good alignment means the binary geometry follows visible material rather
    than background noise.

  - Stratified raw histogram: Shows sampled raw intensities. The orange lines at 28,279 and 56,047 are the 0.5th and 99.5th percentile normalization limits
    —not segmentation thresholds.

  - Raw Z-slice intensity trends: Mean and standard deviation change with material coverage. Periodic standard-deviation peaks are consistent with repeated
    lattice structure.

  - Ensemble disagreement: Red pixels are locations where the three selected segmentation methods did not all agree. Disagreement is concentrated along
    strut boundaries, where partial-volume effects make foreground/background assignment ambiguous.

  The selected segmentation:

  - occupies 11.73% of the analysis volume;
  - retains 99.99% of its foreground in one dominant connected component;
  - has disagreement in 6.21% of all analysis voxels.

  Significance: Global connectivity is strong, and the overlay broadly follows the CT. However, 6.21% disagreement is substantial compared with an 11.73%
  foreground fraction. Thin struts may therefore gain or lose a meaningful fraction of their width—or even their connectivity—depending on the threshold.
  The disagreement image is especially important when interpreting small diameters and short graph branches.

  ## 3. Skeleton and logical graph

  outputs/reports/skeleton_graph_diagnostics.png

  This figure evaluates whether the segmented solid was reduced to a useful centerline network.

  - Central-Z mask + skeleton: White is segmented material; red is its one-voxel-wide skeleton. Ideally, red lines should run near the centers of struts
    without unexplained side branches.

  - Longest retained logical edges + junctions: Blue lines are the 1,800 longest retained graph edges; red points are junction nodes with degree at least
    three. This is only a subset of the 15,682 retained edges, so it is a diagnostic rendering rather than the complete graph.

  The extracted graph contains:

  - 138,996 skeleton voxels;
  - 5,448 logical nodes and 15,682 retained logical edges;
  - one connected component;
  - 1,171 short terminal spurs removed;
  - a cycle rank of 10,235;
  - 96.47% skeleton accounting.

  Significance: The one connected component supports the conclusion that the lattice forms a globally continuous network. But the very dense junction
  cloud, 15,682 edges, and high cycle rank indicate much more small-scale topological complexity than a clean cell-level lattice representation would
  normally suggest. Likely contributors include voxel stair-stepping, segmentation boundary noise, 26-neighbor connectivity, merged material around nodes,
  and the 4× sampling grid.

  Thus, this graph is suitable for describing the CT-derived voxel network, but its individual nodes and edges should not automatically be equated with
  nominal CAD nodes and struts. CAD registration or stronger graph simplification would be needed for that interpretation.

  ## 4. Morphometry diagnostics

  outputs/reports/morphometry_diagnostics.png

  ### Per-edge local diameter

  Each skeleton point samples twice the foreground Euclidean distance transform. Measurements within 8 original voxels of each endpoint are excluded from
  the per-edge summary to reduce junction thickening.

  The huge peak at diameter 8 is the most important observation:

  - the dataset-wide reliable-edge median is 8;
  - its interquartile range is also 8–8;
  - 13,711 of 14,728 edges with a finite median—93.1%—have an exact median diameter of 8.

  Because the analysis-grid spacing is 4, a centerline voxel one sampled voxel from the background has radius 4 and diameter 8.

  Significance: The dominant diameter is an analysis-resolution floor, not convincing evidence that nearly every physical strut has exactly the same
  diameter. Most small struts are under-resolved after 4× decimation.

  ### Distinct diameter estimators

  The scatter plot compares:

  - local median diameter: distance-transform width along the centerline;
  - volume–length equivalent diameter: the diameter of a cylinder having the foreground volume assigned to that edge and the same graph length.

  The dashed line denotes equality. The estimates differ systematically because they measure different things. The volume-based estimate includes
  foreground assigned by nearest-centerline partitioning, including material near bulky nodes, and is affected by how densely adjacent edges are
  represented.

  Significance: Agreement near the diagonal increases confidence that an edge behaves like a roughly cylindrical strut. Large deviations can indicate
  junction contamination, non-circular shape, taper, an incorrect logical edge, or biased volume assignment. These two columns should not be treated as
  interchangeable diameter measurements.

  ### Node thickening ratio

  This is the robust local node diameter divided by the median diameter of its incident struts.

  - A value near 1 means little detected thickening.
  - Values above 1 suggest a node thicker than its incident struts.
  - Values below 1 are geometrically unusual and can signal measurement or graph-assignment problems.

  About 87.6% of measurable nodes lie between 0.9 and 1.1, while 8.6% exceed 1.2.

  Significance: Most nodes appear similar in measured size to their incident struts, but this result is also dominated by the same diameter-8 quantization.
  Therefore, the spike near 1 does not prove that physical nodes lack thickening. The high-ratio tail is more useful for identifying candidate bulky or
  merged nodes for inspection.

  ## 5. Diameter variation along individual edges

  outputs/reports/diameter_along_edge_profiles.png

  This plots local distance-transform diameter along the eight longest edges that passed the notebook’s reliability rule.

  - Flat profiles indicate approximately uniform detected width.
  - Gradual increases or decreases can indicate taper.
  - End increases may be caused by node thickening.
  - Sharp jumps can indicate a path crossing a junction, entering a merged region, or moving between quantized distance-transform levels.
  - The repeated plateaus at values such as 8, 24, 32, 40, and 48 are characteristic of coarse voxel-grid measurement.

  Some edges remain near 24–32, while edges 235 and 239 spend much of their length near 40–49. Those unusually thick paths may represent boundary
  structures, merged material, or paths through node-rich regions rather than ordinary lattice struts.

  Two caveats apply:

  - Although endpoint samples are excluded from the tabulated per-edge diameter summary, this visualization plots the complete cached profile, including
    endpoints.

  - “Reliable” here means the edge has enough non-junction samples and passes basic length/exclusion rules. It does not prove that it corresponds to one
    physically valid strut.

  ## Overall conclusion

  Together, the figures support a connected, successfully segmented bright-material lattice. Their strongest scientific message, however, is that the 4×
  analysis grid is too coarse for precise small-strut morphometry. Connectivity and broad spatial trends are useful, while the exact node count, edge
  count, diameter-8 peak, and node/strut ratios should be treated as resolution-dependent CT descriptors—not ground-truth manufacturing dimensions or a
  recovered nominal CAD topology.
